## Course Syllabus

- **Level:** Preparatory
- **Discipline:** Mathematics
- **Prerequisites:** Algebra I, Algebra II, and Euclidean Geometry
- **Format:** Self-paced, text-based instruction
- **Assessment:** Unit Examinations and Comprehensive Final Examination
- **Calculator Policy:** No calculators unless explicitly permitted

---

## Course Description

Precalculus is a rigorous study of the algebraic, geometric, and analytic structures underlying calculus and higher mathematics.

The course develops fluency in mathematical reasoning, symbolic manipulation, functional relationships, trigonometry, and analytic geometry. Particular emphasis is placed on understanding why mathematical methods work, recognizing structural connections, constructing precise arguments, and solving unfamiliar problems.

Unlike conventional precalculus courses, this course introduces foundational ideas from mathematical analysis, including rigorous treatment of functions, inequalities, sequences, limits, and elementary proof techniques.

The objective is not merely preparation for calculus, but the development of mathematical maturity necessary for advanced undergraduate mathematics.

---

## Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion, students will be able to:

1. Manipulate algebraic expressions with precision and justify transformations.
2. Construct and evaluate mathematical arguments using elementary proof techniques.
3. Analyze functions through their domains, ranges, compositions, inverses, and transformations.
4. Investigate polynomial, rational, exponential, logarithmic, and trigonometric functions.
5. Establish and apply fundamental algebraic and trigonometric identities.
6. Solve equations and inequalities analytically, including those involving parameters.
7. Interpret geometric relationships using coordinates, vectors, and algebraic methods.
8. Work confidently with complex numbers in Cartesian and polar forms.
9. Analyze sequences, series, and elementary recurrence relations.
10. Understand the foundational concepts of limits and continuity.
11. Connect algebraic, geometric, and analytic representations of mathematical objects.
12. Solve challenging, unfamiliar problems through independent reasoning.

# Assessment Structure

## Practice Problems

Every instructional article includes a carefully constructed problem set.

Problems are organized into four difficulty levels:

**Level I — Foundations**

- Tests definitions, essential techniques, and fundamental understanding.
- Requires precise execution of core mathematical procedures.

**Level II — Development**

- Combines multiple concepts and requires nontrivial reasoning.
- Emphasizes mathematical structure and conceptual connections.

**Level III — Challenge**

- Introduces unfamiliar configurations and demanding problems.
- Requires strategic insight, abstraction, and creative application.

**Level IV — Olympiad**

- Features exceptionally difficult problems inspired by mathematical competitions and proof-based university mathematics.
- Emphasizes ingenuity, elegant reasoning, and independent discovery.

Each article contains exactly 20 original free-response problems: 6 Easy (Level I), 6 Medium (Level II), 6 Hard (Level III), and 2 Olympiad (Level IV). Answers appear at the end: final answers only, with only the conclusion or essential proof idea for proof problems. No worked practice solutions are supplied.

Students are encouraged to attempt problems independently before consulting solutions.

---

## Unit Examinations

Each unit concludes with one comprehensive examination.

**Structure:**

- Exactly 30 original free-response problems, exclusively Medium and Hard difficulty.
- Mix of computational, conceptual, and proof-based questions.
- Increasing difficulty throughout the examination.
- Coverage of all major unit objectives.
- A separate verified answer key with concise grading guidance for proof-based questions.

**Conditions:**

- Closed-book attempts recommended.
- No calculators unless explicitly authorized.
- Complete mathematical reasoning required.
- Exact answers preferred whenever appropriate.

**Pacing:** Untimed and self-paced.

---

## Comprehensive Final Examination

Two equivalent final examination variants are provided:

- **Final Examination A**
- **Final Examination B**

Both variants assess the complete course curriculum at equivalent levels of conceptual depth, difficulty, and mathematical rigor.

### Examination Structure

- Exactly 45 original free-response problems per variant, exclusively Medium and Hard difficulty.
- Comprehensive coverage of all 12 units.
- Significant emphasis on connections between topics.
- Computational, conceptual, and proof-based questions.
- Several exceptionally challenging synthesis problems.

**Pacing:** Untimed and self-paced.

Finals are authored only after all course units are complete and verified. Each variant has a separate verified answer key; equivalent blueprints must use genuinely distinct questions.

Both examinations are designed to assess genuine mastery rather than familiarity with standard problem templates.

# Mastery Standards

The course uses a mastery-oriented assessment framework.

| Performance          |     Score | Interpretation                                          |
| -------------------- | --------: | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Exceptional Mastery  |   95–100% | Outstanding command of concepts, techniques, and proofs |
| Advanced Mastery     |    90–94% | Deep understanding with minor deficiencies              |
| Proficient Mastery   |    80–89% | Strong understanding with identifiable weaknesses       |
| Developing Mastery   |    70–79% | Partial mastery requiring targeted improvement          |
| Insufficient Mastery | Below 70% | Substantial conceptual or technical deficiencies        |

**Recommended progression standard:** At least 90% on every unit examination and at least 90% on one comprehensive final examination.

Scores alone do not establish mastery. Students should also be able to explain their reasoning, justify important steps, and solve unfamiliar variations independently.

Students who do not meet the progression standard should revisit weak areas, complete additional problems, and attempt reassessment.

---

# Mathematical Writing Standards

All submitted mathematical work should satisfy the following requirements:

1. **Precision:** Mathematical statements must be unambiguous.
2. **Justification:** Nontrivial conclusions must follow from valid reasoning.
3. **Completeness:** Proofs must address all necessary cases.
4. **Clarity:** Solutions must communicate their logical structure.
5. **Efficiency:** Arguments should avoid unnecessary computation and repetition.
6. **Correctness:** Algebraic transformations must preserve relevant assumptions and domain restrictions.

An unsupported correct answer is not equivalent to a complete mathematical solution.

Students are expected to distinguish numerical evidence from proof, examples from general arguments, and conjectures from established results.
